"Should I be putting the expensive stuff in?" is one of the most common questions drivers ask when they are standing at the bowser choosing between 91, 95 and 98. It feels like the higher number must be better — but that is not how octane actually works.

Those numbers are octane ratings, specifically the Research Octane Number (RON). In Australia, regular unleaded must be a minimum of 91 RON and premium unleaded a minimum of 95 RON, with 98 sitting above that — the parameters are defined in the government's [petrol fuel quality standard](https://www.dcceew.gov.au/climate-change/emissions-reduction/regulating-fuel-quality). This guide explains what the number means and whether paying more for a higher one is actually worth it.

## What Is an Octane Rating?

The octane rating measures a fuel's resistance to premature ignition, known as engine knock or detonation. In a petrol engine, the air-fuel mixture is supposed to ignite only when the spark plug fires at the precise right moment. If the fuel ignites too early due to compression heat alone, it causes a knocking sound and can damage engine components over time.

Higher octane fuel resists this premature ignition better. That is its entire purpose — it is not about energy content, cleaning ability or power output per se.

## RON 91: Standard Unleaded

RON 91 is the minimum octane petrol sold in Australia. It is suitable for naturally aspirated engines designed to run on standard fuel. The vast majority of older Australian cars, many Toyota Corollas, Mazda 3s and similar models run perfectly on 91.

## RON 95: Premium Unleaded

RON 95 is specified as the minimum for many modern turbocharged engines, European cars and some performance variants. These engines operate at higher compression ratios or boost pressures, so they need fuel that resists knock under greater stress.

## RON 98: Ultra Premium

RON 98 is recommended or required for high-performance and luxury vehicles. Cars like the BMW M3, Mercedes-AMG models, and Porsche 911 are tuned to take advantage of 98's knock resistance, allowing the engine control unit to advance ignition timing for peak power and efficiency.

## Will Premium Fuel Help a Standard Car?

If your car specifies RON 91, filling with 98 will not add horsepower or improve fuel economy in any meaningful way. The engine management system is calibrated for 91 and cannot advance timing beyond its design parameters. You are simply paying more for a property your engine cannot exploit.

However, if your car specifies "91 minimum, 95 recommended" — a common specification — then using 95 may yield a small improvement in efficiency and responsiveness, as the ECU can optimise timing with the higher-octane fuel.

## When Premium Fuel Is Essential

If your owner's manual states "minimum 95 RON" or "minimum 98 RON," you must use that grade or higher. Modern engines with knock sensors will retard timing to protect themselves when lower-octane fuel is used, but this reduces power and efficiency. Prolonged use of inadequate octane can cause cumulative engine damage.

## The Additive Question

Premium fuels sometimes contain better detergent packages that help keep fuel injectors and intake valves clean. Some drivers report smoother running after switching to premium for this reason alone. However, all Australian fuels must meet the [minimum standards set by the Fuel Quality Standards Act](https://www.dcceew.gov.au/climate-change/emissions-reduction/regulating-fuel-quality), so the base-grade fuel is not leaving your engine dirty.

## How to Find What Your Car Needs

Check these in order:

1. **The fuel flap.** Many cars have a sticker inside the flap stating the minimum grade.
2. **The owner's manual.** Look in the fuel or specifications section, and read the wording carefully. "Minimum 95 RON" means you must use 95 or higher, while "91 RON, 95 recommended" means 91 is safe and 95 may perform slightly better.
3. **The Green Vehicle Guide.** The government's [Green Vehicle Guide](https://www.greenvehicleguide.gov.au/) lists the fuel type for most models sold in Australia.

If you own an imported car, be aware that a US-market manual quotes octane on a different scale (AKI, the number on American pumps), which reads roughly four to five points lower than the RON figure used in Australia.

## What Happens If You Use the Wrong Grade?

**Lower than required:** A modern engine with knock sensors will retard its ignition timing to protect itself. You lose some power and use more fuel, and the risk of harm grows if you do it repeatedly or under heavy load, such as towing or driving hard on a hot day. If you hear pinging or a light rattle when accelerating, switch back to the correct grade.

**Higher than required:** No harm, but usually no benefit either.

**Mixing grades:** This is safe. If you top up half a tank of 95 with 91, the octane rating simply lands somewhere in between.

## Octane and E10

Ethanol raises octane, and E10 is typically rated at around RON 94. That puts it between 91 and 95. If your car requires 95, E10 usually does not meet the requirement, so stick to U95 or U98. If your car takes 91 and is E10-compatible, E10 is a fine choice. Our [fuel types guide](/guides/fuel-types-explained) explains which cars can use it.

## The Real Cost of Going Premium

In a snapshot of Brisbane-area prices taken in September 2026, the cheapest U95 was about 19 cents a litre above the cheapest U91, and the cheapest U98 was about 22 cents above. Those gaps change from day to day, but they show the typical size of the premium.

At around 20 cents a litre, a 50-litre fill costs about $10 more. A car that uses 1,200 litres a year (8 litres per 100 km over 15,000 km) pays about $240 a year extra. If your car requires premium fuel, that is simply the cost of running it correctly. If it does not, it is money spent for no gain. ## Common Myths

**"Premium fuel gives every car more power."** Only if the engine is designed to use the extra octane. A car tuned for 91 cannot make use of 98.

**"Higher octane means more energy."** No. Octane measures resistance to knock, not energy content.

**"Premium keeps the engine cleaner, so it lasts longer."** Fuel brands market the additive packages in their premium grades, but for a healthy engine running the grade it was designed for, regular servicing matters far more.

**"You should alternate grades to keep the engine happy."** There is no need. Consistently using the grade your manufacturer specifies is all that matters.

## Practical Advice

Check your fuel flap or owner's manual. Use the minimum specified octane or higher. If your car says 91, save money and use 91. If it says 95, do not go below 95. And if it says 98, treat that as a non-negotiable requirement for the health of your engine.
