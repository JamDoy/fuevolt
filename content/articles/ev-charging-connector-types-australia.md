There is a rite of passage for new EV owners: pulling up to a public charger for the first time, staring at the cable, and wondering whether it will even fit your car. With petrol, every nozzle fits every filler neck. With EVs, the plug depends on the manufacturer, the charging speed and the era the car was built — and getting caught out at the wrong connector is a genuinely common early mistake.

The good news is that Australia has largely settled on a small number of standards, so once you know your car's connector you rarely have to think about it again. Four connector types dominate the local market, and government resources like [energy.gov.au's guide to EV charging equipment](https://www.energy.gov.au/electric-vehicles/owning-electric-vehicle/electric-vehicle-charging-equipment) cover them in detail.

## Type 2 (Mennekes)

Type 2 is the standard AC charging connector across Australia and Europe. It features a seven-pin circular plug and supports single-phase charging up to 7.4 kW and three-phase charging up to 22 kW. Nearly every modern EV sold in Australia includes a Type 2 inlet for AC charging — the notable exceptions are the Type 1 cars covered below. When you see a public AC charger — the kind often found in shopping centres and council car parks — it almost certainly uses a Type 2 cable.

Home wall boxes also use Type 2. A typical home installation delivers 7 kW on a single-phase connection, adding roughly 40 kilometres of range per hour of charging.

## CCS2 (Combined Charging System)

CCS2 is the dominant DC fast-charging standard in Australia. It extends the Type 2 connector with two additional DC power pins at the bottom. CCS2 chargers range from 50 kW to 350 kW, enabling rapid charging sessions. A 150 kW CCS2 charger can add around 200 kilometres of range in 15 to 20 minutes on a compatible vehicle.

Most new EVs from Hyundai, Kia, BMW, Mercedes, Volkswagen, Ford, BYD and many others use CCS2 for DC fast charging.

## CHAdeMO

CHAdeMO was developed by Japanese automakers and was one of the first DC fast-charging standards. It uses a separate, bulkier round connector. In Australia, CHAdeMO is primarily used by the Nissan Leaf and the Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV. Many older DC fast chargers include both CCS2 and CHAdeMO plugs, but new installations increasingly offer CCS2 only. CHAdeMO supports up to 100 kW in practice, though most Australian CHAdeMO chargers operate at 50 kW.

## Type 1 (J1772): The Older AC Plug

Type 1 is a five-pin round AC connector that comes from the Japanese and North American markets. In Australia it turns up mainly on the Nissan Leaf and the Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV, which pair it with CHAdeMO for DC fast charging. Public AC chargers here use Type 2, so Leaf and Outlander owners need a Type 1 to Type 2 cable to charge from them. Type 1 is single-phase only, so AC charging tops out at around 7 kW. If you are shopping for a used import, check which AC inlet it has before you buy.

## Tesla

Teslas sold in Australia have used different connectors over time. The Model 3 and Model Y have a single CCS2 inlet that handles both AC charging (Type 2) and DC fast charging, so they work with any CCS2 or Type 2 charger. Earlier Model S and Model X cars use Tesla's own modified Type 2 plug, which combines AC and DC in one port. Tesla Superchargers deliver up to 250 kW DC, and Tesla's Destination Chargers at hotels and restaurants provide 7 to 22 kW AC. Tesla has been opening some Supercharger sites to other brands, so if you drive a non-Tesla EV, check the specific site and Tesla's app before relying on one.

## Choosing a Charger

Before plugging in, confirm your car's connector type. Most public charging services and FueVolt display which connectors each station offers. Most EVs sold new in Australia since around 2020 use Type 2 for AC and CCS2 for DC, which covers nearly every public charger in the country. The main exceptions are the Nissan Leaf and Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV, which use Type 1 and CHAdeMO.

## Do You Need Your Own Cable?

It depends on the charger. Many public AC chargers are untethered, which means you plug your own Type 2 cable into both the charger and your car. Most DC fast chargers are tethered, with the cable permanently attached, so you just pick up the plug and connect it. Many new EVs come with a Type 2 charging cable, but not all do, and some include only a portable cord for a household power point. A proper Type 2 cable is usually one of the first accessories worth buying, and it should be rated for what your car and the charger can deliver.

## Charging Speed Tiers

Charging speed depends on both the charger output and your car's onboard acceptance rate. As [energy.gov.au explains](https://www.energy.gov.au/electric-vehicles/owning-electric-vehicle/how-charge-your-electric-vehicle), slow AC chargers (up to 7 kW) are best for overnight top-ups, fast AC chargers (7 to 22 kW) suit a few hours at a shopping centre, and DC fast chargers (50 kW and above) are designed for highway stops where you need a quick boost.

## Your Car Sets the Limit

A charger's headline number is a ceiling, not a promise. A car with a 50 kW peak charging rate plugged into a 350 kW charger will still only draw 50 kW. Charging also slows as the battery fills, especially past about 80 per cent, and a cold battery charges more slowly than a warm one. On AC, your car's onboard charger sets the speed, and many cars accept only 7 kW or 11 kW even when the charger offers 22 kW. Check your car's maximum AC and DC charging rates in the owner's manual before assuming a faster charger will save you time.

## Reading a Charger Listing

When you look up a station on FueVolt or any charging map, check four things: the connector types offered, the power in kilowatts for each plug, the number of bays, and whether the cable is tethered. A listing describes the equipment at a site, not whether a bay is free or working right now. FueVolt shows charger locations and equipment records rather than real-time bay availability, so for an important stop on a long trip, keep a second charger in mind.

## Quick Connector Reference

- **Type 2:** AC charging, up to 7.4 kW single-phase or 22 kW three-phase. Nearly every EV sold in Australia has this inlet, except Type 1 cars.
- **CCS2:** DC fast charging from 50 kW to 350 kW. Used by most new EVs, including the Tesla Model 3 and Model Y.
- **CHAdeMO:** DC fast charging, usually 50 kW at Australian sites. Found on the Nissan Leaf and Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV.
- **Type 1:** AC charging up to about 7 kW. Found on the Nissan Leaf and Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV, and needs a Type 1 to Type 2 cable at public chargers.
- **Tesla modified Type 2:** AC and DC through one port, on older Model S and Model X cars.

Understanding your connector type and the charger speed available helps you plan stops efficiently and avoid arriving at an incompatible station.
